# Design Options

## Job and boundary

Help the human choose how to solve a consequential design problem, not merely list possibilities or plan the work. Activate for a named invocation or a clear request to explore or compare materially different technical approaches. Relevant decisions may concern ownership, state, trust boundaries, processing strategy, or commitments with material switching costs.

Do not activate just because a request says plan, build, review, architecture, or important; a task exists; alternatives can be imagined; or this skill was used earlier. Implementing an approved direction, saving a decision, breaking agreed work into steps, editing a skill, and a generic plan review do not invite a new options exercise. You may briefly flag a consequential unresolved choice during other work, but do not silently expand scope, repeatedly solicit this workflow, or block ordinary work to use it.

Comparison alone authorizes neither implementation nor persistence, spending, provisioning, deployment, or external changes. The human retains consequential product and technical decisions. This is not an independent or blind review, a general roadmap, or an approval gate.

## Compare and converge

1. **Frame the decision.** Investigate enough relevant evidence to establish the desired outcome, actual constraints, decision owner, prior decisions, and a few criteria that could distinguish the approaches. Separate observed facts, assumptions, and preferences. Do not ask for information you can discover; when a human-owned clarification would change the comparison, ask one consequential question at a time. Establish criteria before ranking; if evidence changes them, explain why and apply the change consistently.
2. **Develop credible alternatives.** Consider relevant differences in ownership, persistence, timing, coupling, computation, trust, or reliance on external systems. Make each serious candidate concrete enough to compare: its flow and ownership, requirements, costs, and where it wins. Include the current arrangement, doing less, or deferral when viable. Do not manufacture a fixed number, rebrand minor variations as alternatives, or include a straw man. If constraints leave one credible approach, explain why the others fail.
3. **Test the same situations.** Apply the same representative workload or user flow, relevant failure, and change scenario to each candidate. Compare only dimensions that affect this decision and expose costs shifted to operators, users, providers, or other components. Treat the human's favorite and your initial favorite fairly; state the strongest credible case against the provisional leader and when a rival wins. Avoid invented scores and false precision.
4. **Investigate the discriminator.** Seek evidence whose answer could change the recommendation; distinguish observations from forecasts. If a bounded experiment would resolve a material uncertainty, identify what it distinguishes and check its authorization before acting. Stop researching when further evidence is unlikely to change the choice; do not make uncertainty an excuse to avoid judgment.
5. **Converge.** Recommend the leading approach, decisive reasons, accepted tradeoff, and evidence or circumstances that would reverse the recommendation. If a consequential unknown leaves a genuine tie, retain the leaders and identify the smallest useful discriminator. Normally give a compact decision brief: decision, credible alternatives, recommendation and tradeoff, and material uncertainty or next decision. Do not leave an unranked menu or ask the human to do the synthesis.

The work ends when the selected decision is settled, paused, or replaced. Deepen its rationale when asked without reopening settled choices absent contrary evidence. Local code organization can be considered directly without automatically invoking another skill; requested persistence and implementation follow their own workflows and permissions.
